Everyone leaves eventually. People move on, priorities change, sometimes a fit that was right stops being right. How a company handles the door on the way out tells you more about it than how it handles the welcome. Here's how we try to do it, whether you're leaving by choice or we're the ones ending it.

## It shouldn't be a surprise

If we're the ones deciding to let someone go, the reasons should already be familiar. We believe in the honest, ongoing kind of [feedback](/handbook/feedback) that means nobody gets blindsided by their own exit. If something isn't working, you'll hear it early, plainly, and with a real chance to change it, long before it becomes a conversation about leaving.

A firing that arrives out of nowhere is usually a failure of the months before it, not a decision made on the day. We'd rather have the uncomfortable conversation early than the devastating one late.

Sometimes it still doesn't work out. That's allowed. Not every good person and every small company are a fit forever, and saying so kindly is better than pretending otherwise.

## What we owe you on the way out

Clarity and dignity, at a minimum. If we're ending it, you get a straight answer about why, said to your face, not buried in process or dressed up as a "reorganization." If you're leaving by choice, you get our genuine thanks and help landing well.

Either way, we handle the practical things without drama: your final pay, whatever's yours to keep, a clean handoff of the work, and references we actually mean. Nobody gets marched out or made to feel like a security threat for having quit.

<Callout type="warn" title="Severance: intent, terms being finalized">
  When we end someone's time here for reasons that aren't serious misconduct, we
  intend to offer real severance, enough to give you room to breathe and find
  the next thing, not a token. The exact terms are being finalized and will live
  here in writing once they are. The intent behind them is not in question.
</Callout>

## How we try to do it

Clear and kind at the same time. Those aren't opposites. The kindest version of hard news is the honest one, delivered like you're talking to an adult you respect, because you are.

That means: real reasons, not corporate fog. A conversation, not an ambush. Enough notice and enough support that you can land on your feet. And no theatre, no walking-you-out spectacle, no pretending it's mutual when it isn't. If we made the call, we'll own it out loud. That's just [how we work](/handbook/how-we-work), applied to the hardest moment.

We'll also be honest about what we got wrong. Often an exit has fault on both sides, or is nobody's fault at all. Saying that plainly is part of the respect.

## Leaving well means you're still part of the story

People who leave Flagon well don't stop being part of it. We build in the [open](https://github.com/flagon-io), so much of what you touched keeps your fingerprints on it whether you're here or not, and we'd rather stay proud of having worked together than pretend it didn't happen.

The industry is small and memory is long. Someone who left years ago might be the person who sends us our next great teammate, or becomes one again themselves. We'd like every exit to be one where that stays possible.

If you're leaving, thank you, honestly. Stay in touch: [hey@flagon.io](mailto:hey@flagon.io) and the [Discord](https://discord.gg/dtYQs6rPXN) doors stay open. And if you're reading this while deciding whether to join, [working here](/handbook/working-here) is the fuller picture of the whole arc.
