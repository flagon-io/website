Starting a software company in this decade is a slightly ridiculous thing to do. There are plenty of companies. There is plenty of software. We started anyway, and it's worth being honest about why.

## Why bother

The short version: we wanted to work somewhere that operated the way we thought a company should, and it was easier to build it than to keep waiting for one to appear.

Most of us have worked at places where the stated values and the actual behavior drifted apart. The handbook said one thing, the calendar said another. Decisions happened in rooms you weren't in, for reasons you never heard, and by the time they reached you they were already law. None of that is unusual. It's just tiring, and it makes for worse software, because the people closest to the work stop bothering to have opinions.

Flagon is the attempt to not do that. Not a manifesto about it, but an actual place, with actual constraints, that we run out loud so you can check whether we mean it.

## The bet on the open

The whole thing rests on one bet: that building in the open is a competitive advantage, not a liability.

Trust isn't something you buy with a marketing budget. It's a track record someone can read. So we publish the track record as we go: the [handbook](/handbook), the roadmap, the reasoning, the misfires. When we get something wrong, the wrong version stays in the git history. That's the point. You can watch us change our minds and see why.

Working this way also makes us sharper. It's hard to quietly adopt a policy you'd be embarrassed to publish. When you have to defend a decision in public, the weak ones fall over before they ship. Openness is partly a values thing and partly just a very good quality filter.

## What "good software" means to us

Good software is software that respects the person using it. It's fast, it does what it says, and it doesn't make you feel stupid. It doesn't nag, it doesn't dark-pattern you into a subscription, and it doesn't treat your attention as inventory to be sold.

We'd rather ship one thing that's genuinely good than ten that are fine. That sounds obvious and almost nobody does it, because "fine" ships faster and mostly gets away with it. We're betting that "genuinely good," done consistently over a long time, compounds into something "fine" never can. More on how that plays out day to day in [how we work](/handbook/how-we-work).

## We decide in the open

Here's the part people find strange: we do our thinking where you can see it, and we don't pretend to certainty we haven't earned.

That's deliberate. Plenty of companies decide behind a curtain and then sell you a roadmap of maybes, a wall of features that may or may not ever arrive. We'd rather show the reasoning. What we're building, why this and not that, what we're choosing between: it's legible, and it's argued in public before it ships. The conviction we're certain about is the *how*. A crew that's honest, careful, and building in the open makes things worth using, because the same habits that make a company legible make its work good. Get the foundation right, in public, and the rest follows. If watching that happen sounds interesting rather than terrifying, we're probably your kind of people.

## The long game

We're not optimizing for a quick exit. There's no clever acquisition we're steering toward, no five-year plan to flip this to someone bigger. The goal is boring and hard: to still be here years from now, still making useful things, still a place worth working at.

That single choice changes everything downstream: how we [pay people](/handbook/compensation), how we [make decisions](/handbook/decisions), how fast we grow. When the time horizon is long, a lot of the usual startup panic just evaporates. We can afford to be patient, because we plan to be around.
