Flagon is built to last, not to sell. That single choice sits underneath almost everything else in this handbook, so it's worth spelling out what it means and what it costs.

## No quick exit

We're not building Flagon to flip it. There's no secret plan to dress the company up, sell it to the highest bidder, and move on. That's a real decision with real consequences, because a company built to sell and a company built to last make different choices at almost every fork in the road.

A company optimizing for an exit will borrow against its own future to make the numbers look good for a buyer: cut the corners nobody checks yet, defer the maintenance, spend down the trust of customers and staff because none of that shows up on the sheet in time to matter. We're optimizing for the opposite. We'd rather be a good, durable company for a long time than an impressive-looking one right before it changes hands.

## Sustainable pace

You can't play a long game at a sprint. A company that runs its people flat out will get a burst of output and then pay it back with interest: burnout, turnover, and the slow rot of quality that comes when everyone's too tired to care about the details. We treat a sustainable pace as a feature of the company, not a perk we grant when there's slack.

That's why [time off](/handbook/time-off) is real here, and why we mean it. Rest isn't the reward for the work, it's part of how the work stays good. Someone who's been going full tilt for a year makes worse decisions than someone who's rested, and worse decisions compound.

  When a decision is hard, we ask which version of it we'd still respect in
  three years. The long game is a reason to be decisive now, not an excuse to
  put everything off forever.
</Callout>

## Decisions you won't resent

Playing the long game changes what you do today. You make the call you won't resent later, even when a shortcut is right there and nobody would notice for a while. You don't ship the thing you'd be embarrassed to demo. You don't take the easy money that quietly costs you the trust you'll need next year.

This is where [our values](/handbook/values) stop being abstract. "Make good things" costs a slipped date sometimes. "The long game" costs turning down deals that would look great this quarter and terrible by the next. If we only lived these when they were free, they'd be decoration. The whole point is that they cost something and we pay it anyway.

## Still here, still good

The goal we're actually optimizing for is simple to say and hard to do: still be here in years, and still be good. Not just alive, plenty of companies survive by becoming things their founders wouldn't recognize. Good and durable, both at once.

That's a demanding target because it rules out the easy versions of success. It rules out the quick sale, the growth-at-all-costs sprint, the corners you cut when you assume someone else will be holding the company by the time the bill comes due. We're planning to be the ones holding it.

The long game isn't slowness dressed up as wisdom, and it isn't an excuse to defer every hard call under the banner of "long term." Patience isn't procrastination with better branding. It's a reason to be decisive now about the things that compound, and to refuse the trades that look smart today and stupid in hindsight. If you catch us borrowing against the future, that's a bug.
